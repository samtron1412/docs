# Camera

## Summary

- Type:
    + SLT (Analog version) (Single Lens Reflex)
    + DSLR (Digital Single Lens Reflex)
        * It uses a mirror system to reflect light through the lens into
          an optical viewfinder (OVF), allowing the photgrapher to see
          what the lens sees in real-time.
        * When you press the shutter, the mirror mechanism moves out of
          the way, allowing light to hit the digital sensor to capture
          the image.
    + Mirrorless
        * It does not use an optical viewfinder and mirror mechanism to
          reflect light.
        * Instead, light travels directly to the digital sensor from the
          lens, creating the image and displaying it on an electronic
          viewfinder.
- Brands
    + Canon (beginner friendlier than Nikon in term of usability)
    + Nikon (professional focus)
    + Sony

##### No RAW option

- HDR setting is turned on, turn it off to use RAW image quality.
- Using Auto Exposure Bracketing (AEB) feature instead
    + This will tells your camera to quickly shoot 3 to 5 separate RAW

# Lens

- Numbers and Values on Lens
    + Focal Length (e.g., 50mm, 18mm-150mm)
        * It dictates the field of view, or how "zoomed in" the image
          is.
        * Smaller numbers mean wide-angle view: 45 or 50 mm focal point
          has a similar field of view as our eyes.
            * Smaller than 45 is for landscape and larger field of view.
            * Bigger than 50 is for portrait and zoomed in (telephoto
              view) such as wildlife/sport)
        * Zoom (variable focal length) vs Prime (fixed)
    + Aperture (e.g., f/1.8, f/3.5-5.6, 1:2.8)
        * The size of the opening that lets light into the camera.
        * Smaller f-number means a larger opening, allowing more light
          in, which is better for low-light situations.
    + Diameter symbol followed by a number: the filter thread size in
      millimeters, indicating which size filters to buy.
- IS (Image Stabilization): internal technology that reduces blur from
  camera shake.
- USM / STM: autofocus motor
    + USM is fast and precise for still shooting
    + STM is smooth and quiet, ideal for video

# Photo Shooting / Taking

Here is how to optimize your Canon R50 for superior results:
1. Settings for Superior Quality

    Shoot in RAW: Enable RAW or C-RAW+JPEG in the menu. RAW files retain much more data, allowing for better editing in Lightroom, whereas iPhones apply heavy, irreversible computational processing.
    Aperture Priority Mode (Av): Use Av mode to control your depth of field. A lower f-number (e.g., f/2.8) creates a shallow depth of field with blurry backgrounds (bokeh) that phones struggle to replicate naturally.
    ISO Settings: Keep ISO as low as possible (ISO 100-800) for cleaner, less grainy images. Only increase it in low light.
    White Balance: Move away from Auto White Balance (AWB) in tricky lighting. Use presets like Cloudy or Daylight for more accurate colors.

2. Essential Lenses
While the kit lens is capable, it has limitations.

    Prime Lenses: The RF 50mm f/1.8 STM is a very affordable upgrade that offers far better low-light performance and sharper images with superior bokeh compared to the 18-45mm kit lens.
    Fast Zoom Lenses: Third-party options like the Sigma 18-50mm f/2.8 offer a constant bright aperture throughout the zoom range.

3. Composition & Focusing

    Use Whole Area AF: Set the autofocus to "Whole Area AF" to scan the entire frame for subjects, which is ideal for portraits.
    Eye Detection: Enable eye detection to ensure focus is always on the subject's eye, which is critical for portraits, notes this YouTube video.
    Use the Gridlines: Enable the grid in your viewfinder to improve composition.

4. Post-Processing

    Editing RAW Files: Use software like Adobe Lightroom to adjust highlights, shadows, and colors, which will make the image quality far exceed the compressed JPEG output of a smartphone.

## Fundamentals

### Genres of Photography

- **Portrait** — people are the subject. Sub-types: headshots, environmental portraits (subject + setting), studio, candid.
- **Landscape** — wide nature scenes, usually no people or tiny people for scale.
- **Macro** — extreme close-ups (flowers, insects).
- **Street** — candid people in public spaces.
- **Documentary / Photojournalism** — telling a true story over time.
- **Wildlife / Sports** — long lenses, fast shutter, fast autofocus.
- **Architecture / Interiors** — wide lenses, careful lines.
- **Still Life / Product** — controlled light on objects.
- **Astrophotography** — night sky, long exposures, tripod required.

The same fundamentals (exposure, light, composition) serve all genres.

### The Exposure Triangle

A photo is light hitting a sensor for a measured time. Three controls decide
how much light and what it looks like:

| Control | What it does | Side effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Aperture** (f/1.8, f/8) | Size of the lens opening | Smaller f-number = more light + shallower depth of field (blurred background) |
| **Shutter speed** (1/200s, 1s) | How long the sensor is exposed | Slower = more light + motion blur |
| **ISO** (100, 800, 6400) | Sensor sensitivity / amplification | Higher = more light + digital noise (grainy) |

- Rules of thumb:
    + Handheld minimum shutter ≈ 1/(focal length × crop factor). On an APS-C
      body with a 50mm lens that's ~1/80s. For moving people use 1/200s or
      faster.
    + Single subject with blurred background: f/1.8–f/2.8 on a 50mm.
    + Two people side by side: f/2.8–f/4 so both eyes are sharp.
    + Landscapes with everything sharp front-to-back: f/8–f/11.
    + Keep ISO as low as light allows. R50 is clean to ISO 1600, usable to
      ISO 6400.
- **Stops**: each control has stops. One stop = doubling/halving the light.
    + f/2.8 → f/4 is one stop less light.
    + 1/100 → 1/200 is one stop less light.
    + ISO 400 → ISO 800 is one stop more light.
    + This lets you trade between them while keeping exposure constant.

### Camera Modes

- **Auto** — camera decides everything. Skip.
- **P (Program)** — camera picks aperture + shutter; you control ISO/WB.
  Training wheels.
- **Av (Aperture Priority)** — you set aperture and ISO, camera picks
  shutter. Default mode for ~80% of working photographers.
- **Tv (Shutter Priority)** — you set shutter, camera picks aperture. For
  motion (sports, kids).
- **M (Manual)** — you set everything. Best when light is constant and
  consistency matters (studio, tripod).

Practical default for the R50: **Av**, ISO **Auto with max 3200**.

### Light — the actual subject of every photograph

"Photography" means *drawing with light*. Light has four properties:

1. **Quantity** — how much. Determines exposure settings.
2. **Quality** — *hard* (small source, sharp shadows, e.g. midday sun) or
   *soft* (large source, gradual shadows, e.g. overcast sky, open shade).
3. **Direction** — front, side, back, top. Side and back light look most
   three-dimensional.
4. **Color temperature** — warm (sunset, ~3000K) to cool (shade/overcast,
   ~7000K). Controlled by white balance.

For portraits the single most important factor is soft, directional light on
the face. Hard overhead midday sun creates raccoon-eye shadows and
squinting.

### Composition

Tools, not laws. Break them with intent.

- **Rule of thirds** — divide the frame 3×3; place important things on the
  lines or intersections.
- **Leading lines** — paths, fences, rows of plants that draw the eye to the
  subject.
- **Framing** — shoot through an arch, foliage, or window.
- **Negative space** — empty area gives the subject room to breathe.
- **Foreground / midground / background** — layered depth makes a flat photo
  three-dimensional.
- **Background check** — before pressing the shutter scan for trees growing
  out of heads, trash cans, bright distractions.
- **Get closer / fill the frame** — most common beginner mistake is standing
  too far back.
- **Eye level** — shoot at the subject's eye level, not from standing height
  looking down.
- **Cut between joints, not at them** — never crop at a wrist, elbow, knee,
  or ankle.

### Focal Length and Perspective

Focal length is not just zoom — it changes *perspective*. Wide lenses
exaggerate distance and distort faces if close. Long lenses compress and
flatter faces.

R50 has a 1.6× crop sensor. Full-frame equivalents:

| Lens | Actual | Equivalent | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| RF-S 18–45mm | 18mm | ~29mm | Wide environmental shots, landscapes |
| RF-S 18–45mm | 35mm | ~56mm | "Normal" view, walking-around |
| RF-S 18–45mm | 45mm | ~72mm | Tighter environmental portraits |
| EF 50mm f/1.8 | 50mm | ~80mm | Classic portrait length, flattering compression |

For tight portraits use the 50mm. For "subject with garden around them" use
the kit at 24–35mm.

### Depth of Field (DoF)

How much of the photo is sharp front-to-back. Controlled by:

- Aperture (wider = shallower)
- Focal length (longer = shallower)
- Subject distance (closer = shallower)

The 50mm f/1.8 wide open, close to the subject, produces creamy background
blur ("bokeh").

### Focus

- Use **Servo AF** (continuous) with **Eye Detection / Face + Tracking**
  enabled. The R50 is excellent at this.
- Half-press the shutter to focus, fully press to take the photo. (Back-button
  focus is an advanced alternative.)
- Always focus on the eye nearer the camera. If the eye is sharp, the photo
  works.

### White Balance

In daylight, **Auto WB** is fine on the R50. If you shoot **RAW**, WB can be
changed after the fact with no quality loss. Set the camera to **RAW + JPEG
(Large)** — JPEG to share quickly, RAW to edit seriously.

### Histogram

A graph on the back of the camera showing brightness distribution. Trust it
more than the screen (which lies in bright sun).

- Right edge jammed against the wall → blown highlights (data lost forever).
- Left edge jammed → crushed shadows.
- Aim for a histogram bunched in the middle without touching either edge.

### Portrait Photography

Three pillars of a good portrait:

1. **Sharp eyes** — non-negotiable. Eye-detect AF handles this.
2. **Flattering light on the face** — soft, slightly from the side, slightly
   from above (but not midday-overhead).
3. **A background that supports, doesn't compete** — either blurred (shallow
   DoF) or meaningfully placed (environmental).

Starting settings for portraits:

- Mode: **Av**
- Aperture: **f/2 or f/2.8** on the 50mm for solo, **f/4** for two people
- ISO: **Auto, max 3200**
- Shutter (camera-set): should land at 1/200s or faster — if not, raise ISO
- AF: **Servo + Face/Eye detect**
- Drive: Single shot, or low-speed continuous for candids
- Metering: Evaluative
- File: RAW + JPEG

Posing basics (general / maternity):

- 3/4 body angle is more flattering than straight-on or full profile.
- Hands matter — give them something to do (on the belly, holding a flower,
  in a pocket). Avoid stiff dangling arms.
- Chin slightly forward and down — eliminates double-chin.
- Weight on the back foot, front foot relaxed.
- Turn shoulders away from camera, face back toward camera.
- Couple shots: forehead-to-forehead, hand-on-belly together, walking,
  laughing, one looking at the other rather than both at the camera.
- Talk to the subject constantly. Real expressions beat posed ones.

### Working in Hard Midday Light

Midday sun is hard, top-down, and unflattering. Tactics:

- **Open shade** — a shaded area that opens to bright sky on one side (under
  a tree canopy near the edge, under a pergola, on the shaded side of a
  building). The sky becomes a giant softbox.
    + Avoid dappled shade (sun spots through leaves cause blotchy faces).
    + Avoid deep shade (light becomes too cool/blue and flat).
- **Backlight** — put the sun behind the subject. Hair gets a glowing rim,
  face is in even shade. Watch for lens flare; shade the lens with a hand or
  hat. Expose for the face.
- **Use the environment as a diffuser** — greenhouses with translucent roofs
  are giant softboxes. Plan around shaded paths.
- **If you must shoot in sun** — position so sun is behind or 90° to the
  side, never front-and-above. Have the subject look down briefly, then up
  on cue. Use a white shirt or reflector to fill the shadow side of the
  face.

### Practice Path (5–7 days)

1. **Camera familiarity** — set Av, RAW+JPEG, Auto ISO max 3200, Servo AF +
   Eye Detect, grid display ON. Practice swapping lenses fast.
2. **Exposure triangle drill** — shoot the same subject at f/1.8, f/4, f/8,
   f/16 (compare DoF). In Tv shoot moving subjects at 1/30, 1/250, 1/2000
   (compare motion). In M, lock ISO 100, set "correct" exposure, then
   over-/under-expose by 1 and 2 stops.
3. **Light study** — photograph the same subject at sunrise, midday, and
   sunset. Photograph a person in direct sun, open shade, backlit, and
   indoors by a window.
4. **Composition walk** — go out with the 50mm only. Restrict to one focal
   length to force movement. Practice rule of thirds, leading lines,
   framing, foreground layering.
5. **Tripod + self-timer** — practice the couple-shot workflow. Set up
   tripod, frame, focus on a stand-in, then trigger via 10s timer + 5-shot
   burst, or via the Canon Camera Connect phone app.
6. **Portrait practice with a real subject** — one hour in good light. Try
   the posing list. Get comfortable directing.
7. **Gear prep & review** — charge battery, format SD card, clean lenses,
   pack the bag. Review the week's photos and pick keepers.

### Per-Shot Checklist

Before pressing the shutter, in 5 seconds:

1. **Light** — where is it on the face? Is the subject squinting?
2. **Background** — anything weird growing out of heads? Bright distractions?
3. **Focus point** — is eye-detect locked on the right eye?
4. **Settings** — aperture for this shot, shutter speed fast enough?
5. **Frame** — subject on a third? No limbs cut at joints?

### Couple-Shot Workflow

- Set up tripod, compose leaving room where you'll stand.
- **Av, f/4, ISO Auto.**
- Use the **Canon Camera Connect** app on phone for live preview + remote
  shutter, or **10s timer + 5-shot burst**.
- Take 3 versions of every pose — first ones are stiff, later ones relax.

### Packing Checklist

- Essential:
    + Camera body, charged
    + Spare battery
    + Lenses + adapter
    + 2× SD cards, formatted
    + Microfiber cloth + blower
    + Tripod
    + Phone with Canon Camera Connect installed and paired
    + Lens hood (helps with flare)
- For subject comfort (especially pregnant subject in heat):
    + Water and snacks
    + Sun hat, sunscreen
    + Light layer (greenhouses can be cold or hot)
    + Folding stool if the location is large
- Nice to have:
    + White poster board or 5-in-1 reflector for fill light
    + Polarizing filter for the wide zoom (cuts glare on leaves, deepens
      sky)
    + Notebook to jot what you learned

### Post-Trip Review

1. Import all RAW + JPEG to a computer.
2. First pass: flag favorites quickly (gut reaction).
3. Second pass: of favorites, ask *why* each works. Light? Composition?
   Expression? Write it down.
4. Of rejects, ask *why* — this is where learning happens.
5. Edit keepers in Lightroom (paid) or Darktable (free). Basic edits only:
   exposure, WB, contrast, crop, slight sharpening. Don't over-edit.
6. Print 2–3 favorites. Photos live differently on paper.

### Long-Term Learning Path

1. **Light** (deeper) — *Light, Science & Magic* by Hunter/Biver/Fuqua.
2. **Composition** (deeper) — *The Photographer's Eye* by Michael Freeman.
   Reverse-engineer photos you love.
3. **Editing** — Lightroom basics via free YouTube series.
4. **One genre at a time** — pick portrait first, dive deep for 2–3 months.
   Then add landscape.
5. **Shoot constantly.** Volume + intentional review beats theory. Aim for
   one shoot a week.
6. **Get critique** — r/photocritique, local clubs.

Recommended free YouTube channels: *Sean Tucker* (philosophy), *Mango
Street* (couples/portrait), *Thomas Heaton* (landscape).

### Verification (after a shoot)

You should be able to say:

- I shot in Av the whole day and understood why every aperture mattered.
- I avoided direct overhead sun on faces; I used open shade or backlight.
- I have at least 3 portraits with tack-sharp eyes.
- I have couple photos that aren't selfies — framed and lit deliberately.
- I have wide environmental shots that show the location and the subject in
  it.
- I can name what went wrong on throwaways and what went right on keepers.

## Common Issues

- Motion blur
    + A function of shutter speed and your subject's movement
    + Faster shutter speed will help with capturing moving subjects.
- Subject separation: a function of your depth of field and the distance
  between your subject and the background.
    + A shallower depth of field, then a wider aperture (lower f value:
      1.4 - 4): sharp subject and a blurry background (ideal for
      portraits)
    + High f-number (f/11-22): a narrow aperture with a deep depth of
      field, keeping both the foreground and background in focus (ideal
      for landscpes)

# Photo Editing and Organizing

Image / photo editing is the process of altering digital or traditional
photographs to enhance, correct, or creatively modify them, ranging from
simple adjustments like lighting and cropping to advanced
manipulation. Key focus areas include improving exposure, correcting
white balance, enhancing colors, and removing distracting elements to
improve the image's story and aesthetic quality.

Key Areas to Focus On in Photo Editing:

- Lighting and Exposure: Adjust the brightness or darkness of the photo, bringing out details in shadows and highlights, and ensuring the contrast is balanced.
- Color Correction & White Balance: Adjust the Temperature (warm/cool) and Tint (green/magenta) to match the scene's actual lighting or create a desired mood.
- Composition and Framing: Use cropping to remove unwanted elements, improve the image's composition, and focus attention on the main subject.
- Correction and Cleaning: Remove, or spot retouch, distracting background objects, blemishes, or dirt to create a cleaner image.
- Enhancing Details: Use sharpening tools to make images clearer, and subtly adjust clarity or texture, ensuring not to overdo it, which can cause unnatural results.

Essential Editing Tips:

- Use a Histogram: Use this to analyze and guide the proper exposure levels of your photo.
- Be Subtle: Overediting, or overusing settings like contrast and saturation, can reduce image quality.
- Set a Goal: Determine what story the photo is trying to tell before beginning the editing process.
- Focus on the Subject: Ensure your edits bring attention to the primary focus of the picture.

## Software

- Paid:
    + Adobe Lightroom: bulk editing
    + Adobe Photoshop: focused editing
- Open Source:
    + Darktable: alternative to Lightroom
        * It can import photos to a pre-defined folder structure using
          metadata. (organization)
        * It has culling feature. (culling)
        * Batch editing: easily copy and paste edits and presets from
          one photo to others.
        * Non-destructive: edits are saved as instruction files; your
          original raw image is never permanently altered.
        * Cannot handle layers, pixel-level manipulatio  (like
          head-swaps), or custom graphics/text.
    + GIMP: alternative to Photoshop
        * Best for deep image manipulation, pixel perfection, and
          creative artistry.
        * Focused editing to one photo.
        * Poor library management; you edit files one by one, making it
          very slow for processing many photos.
    + DigiKam: maybe better at culling (review to select best photos)

# File and Folder Organization

Folder structure is for browsing and backup. For search by face / place /
object, rely on EXIF + a tool (Darktable, digiKam, Lightroom). Don't try
to make folders do search's job.

## Recommendation: time-first, with a descriptive suffix

`YYYY/YYYY-MM-DD_<event-or-place>[_<extra>]`

```
Photos/
  2026/
    2026-06-05_kyoto-arashiyama/
    2026-06-12_home_baby-ultrasound/
    2026-07_daily/                     # catch-all for ad-hoc phone shots
  2025/
    2025-12-24_christmas_parents-house/
```

- Why time-first beats location- or event-first
    + Every file has a reliable EXIF date; not every file has GPS or a
      clean "event."
    + Sorting by name = sorting by date. Backups (rsync) diff cleanly
      because old folders are immutable.
    + Trips span cities, events span days — time always sorts cleanly,
      the others don't.
    + Daily phone snaps without a clear event fit a `YYYY-MM_daily/`
      bucket.
- Why the descriptive suffix
    + Lets the folder name carry place + event for at-a-glance
      recognition without breaking chronological order.
    + Keep it short (2–4 words), lowercase, hyphens within words,
      underscores between fields. Stable across OSes and shells.

## Conventions

- **Date = day shooting started.** Multi-day trips stay in one folder
  (`2026-09-10_hokkaido-trip/`) with optional `day1_sapporo/`,
  `day2_otaru/` inside.
- **Merge phone + camera by date.** One folder per shoot tells the full
  story of a day. Distinguish by filename prefix (`IMG_`, `DSC_`) or
  optional `raw/`, `jpg/`, `video/` subfolders if volume warrants.
- **Catch-all** for phone-only daily shots: `YYYY-MM_daily/`. Prevents
  the "where do random snaps go" paralysis.

## Tooling

Auto-create dated folders on import with exiftool:

```
exiftool '-Directory<DateTimeOriginal' -d '%Y/%Y-%m-%d' /path/to/card
```

Then rename the day folder to add the suffix. Darktable's import can do
the same (see Software section above).

# Backup Strategy

- https://creativeraw.com/secure-backup-photos-landscape-one/
- https://creativeraw.com/secure-backup-photos-landscape-two/
- https://creativeraw.com/secure-backup-photos-landscape-three/
- Follow 3-2-1 rule: 3+ copies, using 2 different types of media (HDD
  and SSD) with at least one copy being in a separate location (cloud or
  another server)
    + 2 cold storage (2 HDD): no sync, just copy into it, and read from
      it when we want to use it to restore the original
    + 1 hot storage (1 SSD): this contains the working copy of the RAW,
      editing software such as Darktable and GIMP import RAW files from
      this storage and working on top of it.
    + 1 cloud storage (Amazon Photo for prime member, for videos using
      Google 2TB plan but probably only for a few RAW)
- Isolate and preserve the original raw files so you can always revert
  back to the master file if necessary (3-2-1 strategy above)
    + After a photoshoot, copy the RAW files from SD cards to back up
      storage right away (under your file structure — see *File and
      Folder Organization*)
    + Copy to 2 cold storage and 1 hot storage, and then upload to
      cloud.
- Treat your Darktable / Lightroom catalog file / Photoshop PSD file
  with the same importance as your raw files and back it up accordingly.
    + Using Google Drive / Drop Box to sync catalog and working files
      across devices and also back up.
